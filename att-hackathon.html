The Challenge

The AT&T Hackathon Mexico challenged teams to design and build innovative connected solutions capable of improving people's lives through technology — within a tight competition window.

Our team focused on a problem with clear social urgency: elderly and vulnerable patients who may require rapid medical intervention when critical health conditions arise have no affordable, accessible way to signal distress and automatically reach caregivers.

Every minute of delay in a medical emergency increases risk. We asked: what would it take to reduce that delay to seconds using connected technology?

The Solution — EMA-IOT

We built EMA-IOT (Emergency Medical Aid), a connected health monitoring platform designed to support at-risk patients through continuous vital sign monitoring and automated emergency response.

The platform was designed to:

  • Continuously monitor patient vital signs via a wearable device.
  • Detect abnormal conditions in real time through threshold analysis.
  • Trigger emergency alerts automatically when thresholds are exceeded.
  • Notify caregivers and family members through SMS.
  • Provide visibility into patient status at any time.

The goal was not to replace medical care — it was to ensure that emergencies were detected and escalated immediately rather than discovered too late.

Lessons Learned

Although the specific technologies used in the prototype have evolved significantly since 2016, the experience reinforced lessons that continue to shape how I approach product development and engineering today.

1
Rapid validation forces clarity. A hackathon gives you no time to over-engineer. The discipline of building something demonstrable in hours — not weeks — is one of the most valuable exercises in cutting through ambiguity to what actually matters.
2
MVPs win on problem clarity, not technical sophistication. We didn't win because of the technology we used. We won because the problem was clearly defined and the solution was immediately understandable. Judges respond to solutions that make the problem obvious.
3
Cross-functional teams outperform single-discipline groups. The team's different perspectives — design, product, engineering — produced a more complete solution than any individual could have built alone under pressure.
4
Communicating technical solutions is a skill of its own. Winning required not just building the solution but explaining it clearly to non-technical judges. That experience shaped how I present architecture and system design decisions to stakeholders today.
5
Technology is most powerful when applied to real human problems. Healthcare and civic technology — the domains where I've done my most meaningful personal work — both demand that engineering decisions start with the person the system is supposed to help.
